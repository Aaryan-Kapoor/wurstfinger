# Emoji mode: finding an emoji by feeling

The emoji mode replaces the letter grid with nine **feelings**. You do not
search a list: you think "that is a bit angry, but tired" and flick toward
where that feeling lives. Nothing scrolls, and the whole map is on screen.

Enter it with a **swipe down-right on the `123` key**. Leave with a **tap on the
`abc` key** (the layout's own label: `אבג`, `абв`, ...). The mode has its own
utility column: the globe (next keyboard, hide, language), the `abc` key, delete,
space and return, all working as they do on the letters. Page two says so on the
`abc` key (`abc²`).

## 1. The two rules

**Where a feeling lives (the compass).** The nine keys are laid out like the two
axes psychologists use to chart affect (Russell's circumplex): left is sour,
right is sweet, top is loud, bottom is quiet.

```
   Anger      Surprise   Joy
   Worry      Playful    Love
   Sadness    Neutral    Approval
```

Anger and Joy are the loud ends of the sour and sweet columns, Sadness and
Approval the quiet ends, Surprise and Neutral the loud and quiet ends of the
middle (neither sour nor sweet), Playful and Love sit on the equator.

**How a key reads (the same on all nine).** A key is a 3x3 map of one feeling:

```
   more, another face     more              more, a thing
   another face           THE FAMILY        a hand, heart or symbol
   calmer, another face   calmer            calmer, a thing
```

- **Rows are intensity.** A flick up is louder than the tap, a flick down is
  quieter. (The same direction as the compass, so "up" always means "more".)
- **Columns are kind.** The middle column is the family's own line, with the
  tap as its most representative emoji. The left column holds other faces of
  the same feeling. The right column holds a hand, a heart or a symbol for it.
- **A return swipe** (out and back) goes **further in the same direction**: the
  second-level variant of that cell (up and back is the loudest, down and back
  the quietest). It is the same gesture the letters use for capitals.
- **A circle** (either way round) **turns the page**. Page two holds nine more
  per family in the same map; a circle on it turns back.
- **A hold** types what the tap types (see "Long press").

The tenth key, bottom left, is **Recent**: the emoji typed last, with the newest
on the tap and the others clockwise from "up" in order of recency. It reads
like the other keys (tap, flick, circle for page two).

## 3. Why these members (research)

**Frequency.** The prototypes and the first ring of every key come from how often
people actually type the emoji: the Unicode Consortium's emoji frequency list
(`unicode.org/emoji/frequency.html`; its page says it is an archive of earlier
years' data, ranks are by position in its list). Of the ten most frequent emoji
all ten are on the map, 29 of the first 30 (the miss is the text-style
duplicate of the red heart), 47 of the first 50 and 87 of the first 100. The
most frequent ones sit on the taps and the first ring: 😂 (1) is the joy tap,
❤️ (2) the love key's right flick, 😍 (3) the love tap, 🤣 (4) joy up, 😊 (5)
joy down, 🙏 (6) approval down, 💕 (7) love up-right, 😭 (8) sadness up,
😘 (9) love up, 👍 (10) the approval tap. Rank 11 to 100 are spread over the first
page and its return swipes. The recent key starts out as the first nine of that
list (😂 ❤️ 😍 🤣 😊 🙏 💕 😭 😘), so it is useful before anything was typed.

**Feeling.** The grouping and the intensity order were checked against the Emoji
Sentiment Ranking (Kralj Novak, Smailović, Sluban, Mozetič, *Sentiment of
Emojis*, PLoS ONE 10(12), 2015; data v1.0 from CLARIN.SI, handle 11356/1048:
sentiment score = (positive - negative) / occurrences, from tweets in 13 European languages labelled by human annotators). The
occurrence-weighted mean score of each family's first page is +0.70 love, +0.49
approval, +0.42 playful, +0.33 joy on the sweet side and -0.22 anger, -0.09
sadness on the sour side, with surprise (+0.04) and neutral (+0.01) in the
middle. Two honest limits: tweet sentiment is not affect, so the worry family
(+0.17) comes out sweeter than its place because people use 😅 and 🤞 as relief,
and the ranking dates from 2015, long before most of the emoji on page two existed. The five families that match Ekman's basic emotions
(anger, fear, sadness, joy, surprise) are the five corners and edges of the
map; Russell's circumplex (*A circumplex model of affect*, J. Personality and
Social Psychology 39, 1980) supplies the two axes. The remaining four are the
frequent uses that are not basic emotions: love and approval (the two biggest
families by frequency), playful (the wink and tongue faces) and neutral (🤔 🙄 😐 🥱,
thinking, doubt and tired).

**Names and versions.** Every emoji was checked against Unicode's
`emoji-test.txt` for Emoji 15.0: present as fully qualified, introduced in
version 15.0 or earlier, one grapheme cluster. The typed text is the fully
qualified form (with the emoji selector U+FE0F where it exists), so ❤️ and ☀️ draw
as emoji everywhere.

## 4. Decisions

**Skin tones: one preference, not in the base set.** The map holds every hand
in its default (yellow) form. Settings › General › Emoji Skin Tone picks one of
six tones, shown as the 👍 in each; `EmojiSkinTone` then turns the emoji that
have tone variants into base plus Fitzpatrick modifier **when the pages are
built** (`EmojiLayouts.modes(tone:)`), not on each keystroke. Building is where
it belongs: the hint, the guide card, VoiceOver and the typed text all read the
same binding, so the key shows what it types, and a keystroke does exactly
what it did before (0 allocations, bench rows 8q). A base and its modifier are
one grapheme cluster, and a test holds every emoji of the map in every tone to
that. Which emoji take a tone is `Emoji_Modifier_Base` of the single-scalar
emoji (33 of the 234: the hands, 🙇 🤷 🤦 💃 🕺 🧘 🛌 and the like), checked
against the variants listed in `emoji-test.txt` for Emoji 15.0; faces, hearts
and sequences are left alone. The recent key holds what was typed, in the tone
it was typed in. The view model reads the preference when settings reload and
rebuilds the pages when it changed (and on entering the mode, for a freshly
loaded layout).

**Long press: it types what the tap types.** The hold is an opt-in setting
(off, digits, capitals) and the engine arms no timer when it is off, so a
hold cannot be the way to anything essential. It must still be bound: an
unbound hold falls through to the numeric mode's ghost key and would type the
key's digit among the emoji.

**Recents.** The recent key is nine emoji in the same 3x3 shape. It is read from
the shared defaults when settings reload (never per keystroke), kept in memory
while the mode is up (an in-place move-to-front of nine small strings, no
allocation), put on the key **when the mode is entered** (so the key never
moves under the thumb mid-visit) and written back **when the mode is left** (once
per visit). Nothing is remembered in a secure or sensitive field (the field
traits the gesture trail already respects). Stored under the `emojiRecents` key;
anything that is not exactly one emoji, a repeat or beyond nine is dropped when
read, so a hand-edited value cannot put text on a key.

**VoiceOver.** The decision is to let the system speak the emoji and name only
what it cannot. VoiceOver reads an emoji by its CLDR name *in the user's
language*; a table of English short names (`Unicode.Scalar.Properties.name` is
upper case and ASCII-only) would read English in every language, and a
translated table is 234 names times 22 languages that would then go stale with
every Unicode release. So:

- the **key** is named by its family (localized, one of nine strings) and its tap:
  "Joy, 😂", which VoiceOver reads as "Joy, face with tears of joy";
- every other emoji is a **custom action** named by itself and its gesture
  ("🤣, swipe up"; return swipes add "and back"), generated like the letters'
  with one change: an emoji is not spelled out (a ZWJ sequence such as ❤️‍🔥 would
  be read piece by piece);
- the **page turn** is the one named action per key ("More emoji" and "Main
  emoji"), the entry gesture on the `123` key is "Emoji", the recent key is
  "Recent emoji".

Gesture typing under VoiceOver (`SpokenFeedback`) speaks the target as the
finger moves, which for an emoji is the emoji itself, and so is localized too.

**Compatibility.** Only Emoji 15.0 and earlier (iOS 17 draws them; 🫨 from 15.0 is
the newest in use). A test lists the Emoji 15.1 and 16.0 additions as a deny
list and holds every binding to one grapheme cluster.

**Labels are never hidden.** The "hide letters / symbols" toggles are for
practising the letter layout from memory; an emoji is the content of the mode,
so `LabelCategory` treats it as functional (always visible).

**Size and fit.** An emoji is as wide as it is tall and nine share a key, so
emoji are sized as a fraction of the key (`KeyTypography.emojiHintFraction`
0.19, `emojiCenterFraction` 0.28), not from the letter sizes, which are clamped
(on an iPad the centre stopped growing while the hints did, until the
prototype was the size of its neighbours). Then `HintFit` scales down any hint
or centre glyph whose ink would not fit its cell, never below 0.7, so nothing
runs into its neighbour; it is the same rule for every script (it was found
with Arabic and Persian, where three letters in a row read as one blob). It
measures with Core Text and caches per text and size, so the `Canvas`, the
view-stack renderer and the centre label use one number and a redraw during
an animation shapes nothing.

`EmojiInkAudit` is the evidence: it renders every glyph the way the key draws
it, reads the ink back from the pixels and reports overflow and any two
glyphs of a key closer than a point. `swift run WurstfingerSnapshots
--emoji-audit` prints it for five key sizes (all six tones) and for the letter
keys of every layout; `EmojiInkAuditTests` pins the result. A glyph the
system's emoji font itself draws incomplete (🫡 is cropped at its advance on
the macOS this was written on) is invisible to it, because cropped ink is ink.

Changing the map: edit the `families` table in `EmojiLayouts.swift`. Keep each
cell at its intensity and in its column, keep every emoji on the map once and
one grapheme cluster of Emoji 15.0 or earlier. `EmojiFeelingMapTests` fails on a
duplicate, a count, a newer emoji and a moved family.

## 6. What it costs

The pages are built once per back-key label for the process and shared by every
layout (`EmojiLayouts.sharedModes`), and so are their compiled tables
(`EmojiLayouts.compiled`): the mode costs about 1 KB of heap per layout
(`docs/performance.md`, row 9a). The tables follow a definition's pages only
when they differ from the shared ones.
The recent key's rebuild on entry compiles two modes of eleven keys. On the
keystroke: one string comparison per key outside the emoji mode, and in it the
check of one binding and an in-place list update (`WurstfingerBench --alloc`,
rows 8q and 8q-i).
